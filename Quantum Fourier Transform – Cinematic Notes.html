1. Why a Fourier Transform?

A Fourier transform changes the basis in which we describe data. Instead of asking “what is the value at each position?”, it asks “which frequencies are hiding inside this signal?” Think of a musical chord: your ear hears one sound, but a Fourier transform reveals the individual notes.

Computational basis (CB)
States \(\ket{0},\ket{1},\dots,\ket{N-1}\). This is the “position” description.
Fourier basis
States that carry phase patterns. For one qubit these are \(\ket{+}\) and \(\ket{-}\), produced by the Hadamard gate: \(\ket{0}\to\ket{+}\), \(\ket{1}\to\ket{-}\).
Integral transform
A function \(f(x)\) is mapped to \(\sum_y K(x,y)\,f(y)\) with a kernel \(K(x,y)\). For the QFT, the kernel is a complex exponential.

2. Definition of the QFT

For \(n\) qubits, the dimension is \(N=2^n\). The QFT maps each basis state \(\ket{x}\) to a uniform superposition with rotating phases:

\[\QFT\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi i\,xy/N}\ket{y}\]

Let \(\omega_N=e^{2\pi i/N}\) be the N-th root of unity, since \(\left(e^{2\pi i}\right)^{1/N}=\omega_N\) and \(\omega_N^{N}=1\). Then:

\[\QFT\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1}\left(\omega_N\right)^{xy}\ket{y}\]

Here \(x\) and \(y\) are ordinary decimal numbers labelling basis states, and \(xy\) is simple multiplication. Key facts: (i) every output amplitude has magnitude \(1/\sqrt{N}\), (ii) only the phase changes with \(y\), (iii) the QFT is a unitary matrix (reversible), and (iv) \(\QFT\ket{0}\) is the uniform superposition.

3. The One-Qubit Case (N = 2)

With \(n=1\), \(N=2\) and \(\omega_2=e^{i\pi}=-1\):

\[\QFT\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big[\ket{0}+e^{2\pi i x/2}\ket{1}\Big]=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big[\ket{0}+(-1)^{x}\ket{1}\Big]\] \[\begin{aligned}x=0&:\ \frac{\ket{0}+\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}=\ket{\tilde 0}=\ket{+}\\ x=1&:\ \frac{\ket{0}-\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}=\ket{\tilde 1}=\ket{-}\end{aligned}\]

Hence the one-qubit QFT is exactly the Hadamard gate. For a general state \(\ket{\psi}=\alpha\ket{0}+\beta\ket{1}\) (by linearity):

\[\QFT\ket{\psi}=\alpha\ket{+}+\beta\ket{-}=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sqrt2}\ket{0}+\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sqrt2}\ket{1}\]

4. Circuit Diagram (3 qubits, animated flow)

Each qubit gets one Hadamard, followed by controlled phase rotations from the lower qubits, where \(R_k=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&e^{2\pi i/2^k}\end{pmatrix}\). Gate count: \(n\) Hadamards + \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\) controlled rotations (+ \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\) swaps), so the QFT costs \(O(n^2)\) gates, while the classical FFT needs \(O(n\,2^n)\) operations.

Product form (why the circuit works)

\[\QFT\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bigotimes_{l=1}^{n}\Big(\ket{0}+e^{2\pi i\,x/2^{l}}\ket{1}\Big)\]

6. Real-Life Cinematic Stories

🔐 Breaking Codes (Shor)
RSA security rests on the difficulty of factoring. Shor’s algorithm finds a hidden period using the QFT, turning an impossible-looking heist into a feasible one.
🎼 The Orchestra
A messy sound wave is a chord. The Fourier view lists every note; the QFT does this for quantum amplitudes in a single, reversible step.
🧪 Molecule Energies
Quantum phase estimation uses the inverse QFT to read the energy of a molecule from a phase, which is key to drug and battery design.
📡 Radar & Deep Space
Spotting a faint periodic signal in noise is a frequency-finding task, the classic home of Fourier methods.
🎬 Film Restoration
Flicker in old films is periodic. Moving to the frequency domain isolates it, just as the QFT isolates periodic structure in a quantum state.
💹 Finance
Quantum amplitude estimation (built on the QFT) can speed up option pricing and risk estimation.

7. Numericals with Step-by-Step Solutions

Q1. Find \(\QFT\ket{0}\) and \(\QFT\ket{1}\) for \(N=2\).
Step 1: \(\omega_2=e^{i\pi}=-1\).
Step 2: \(\QFT\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{0}+(-1)^x\ket{1}\big)\).
Step 3: \(x=0\Rightarrow\frac{\ket{0}+\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}=\ket{+}\) and \(x=1\Rightarrow\frac{\ket{0}-\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}=\ket{-}\).
Same as the Hadamard gate.
Q2. For \(\ket{\psi}=0.6\ket{0}+0.8\ket{1}\) compute \(\QFT\ket{\psi}\) and the measurement probabilities.
Here \(\alpha=0.6,\ \beta=0.8\).
\[\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sqrt2}=\frac{1.4}{1.4142}\approx0.990,\qquad\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sqrt2}=\frac{-0.2}{1.4142}\approx-0.141\] \(P(0)=0.990^2\approx0.980\), \(P(1)=0.141^2\approx0.020\). Sum \(=1\) ✔ (unitarity preserved).
Q3. Write the QFT for \(N=4\) and find \(\QFT\ket{1}\).
Step 1: \(n=2,\ N=4,\ \omega_4=e^{2\pi i/4}=i\), so \(\QFT\ket{x}=\frac12\sum_{y=0}^{3}i^{xy}\ket{y}\).
Step 2: for \(x=1\), the powers \(i^{y}\) for \(y=0,1,2,3\) are \(1,\ i,\ -1,\ -i\). \[\QFT\ket{1}=\tfrac12\big(\ket{0}+i\ket{1}-\ket{2}-i\ket{3}\big)\] Similarly \(\QFT\ket{2}=\tfrac12(\ket{0}-\ket{1}+\ket{2}-\ket{3})\) and \(\QFT\ket{3}=\tfrac12(\ket{0}-i\ket{1}-\ket{2}+i\ket{3})\).
Q4. \(N=4\). Apply the QFT to \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})\) and find all probabilities.
Step 1: \(\QFT\ket{0}=\frac12(1,1,1,1)\) and \(\QFT\ket{1}=\frac12(1,i,-1,-i)\).
Step 2: add and multiply by \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\): \[\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\big(2,\ 1+i,\ 0,\ 1-i\big)\] Step 3: \(|1+i|^2=|1-i|^2=2\). So \(P(0)=\frac{4}{8}=\frac12\), \(P(1)=\frac28=\frac14\), \(P(2)=0\), \(P(3)=\frac14\). Total \(=1\) ✔.
Q5. \(N=8\). Find the amplitude of \(\ket{5}\) in \(\QFT\ket{3}\).
Here \(\omega_8=e^{i\pi/4}\). Amplitude \(=\frac{1}{\sqrt8}\,\omega_8^{3\cdot5}=\frac{1}{\sqrt8}\,\omega_8^{15}\).
Since \(\omega_8^{8}=1\): \(\omega_8^{15}=\omega_8^{7}=e^{7\pi i/4}=\frac{1-i}{\sqrt2}\). \[\text{Amplitude}=\frac{1-i}{\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt8}=\frac{1-i}{4},\qquad\left|\frac{1-i}{4}\right|=\frac{\sqrt2}{4}=\frac{1}{\sqrt8}\ ✔\]
Q6. Verify that \(\QFT\ket{0}\) and \(\QFT\ket{1}\) are orthogonal for \(N=4\).
\[\langle\QFT 0\,|\,\QFT 1\rangle=\tfrac14\big(1\cdot1+1\cdot i+1\cdot(-1)+1\cdot(-i)\big)=\tfrac14(1+i-1-i)=0\ ✔\] The columns of the QFT matrix are orthonormal, so it is unitary.
Q7. \(N=4\). What is the QFT of the uniform state \(\frac12(\ket{0}+\ket{1}+\ket{2}+\ket{3})\)?
The uniform state equals \(\QFT\ket{0}\), so we need \(\QFT\,\QFT\ket{0}\). The amplitude of \(\ket{y}\) is \[\frac14\sum_{x=0}^{3}i^{xy}\] For \(y=0\) this is \(\frac44=1\). For \(y\ne0\) the four powers of \(i\) sum to \(0\). Result: \(\ket{0}\). (In general \(\QFT^2\ket{x}=\ket{-x \bmod N}\).)
Q8. Use the product form to write \(\QFT\ket{3}\) for \(n=2\).
Factor 1: \(\ket{0}+e^{2\pi i\cdot3/2}\ket{1}=\ket{0}-\ket{1}\). Factor 2: \(\ket{0}+e^{2\pi i\cdot3/4}\ket{1}=\ket{0}-i\ket{1}\). \[\tfrac12(\ket{0}-\ket{1})(\ket{0}-i\ket{1})=\tfrac12\big(\ket{00}-i\ket{01}-\ket{10}+i\ket{11}\big)\] This equals \(\tfrac12(\ket{0}-i\ket{1}-\ket{2}+i\ket{3})\), matching Q3 ✔.
Q9. How many gates does a 10-qubit QFT need, compared with a classical FFT?
Hadamards: \(10\). Controlled rotations: \(\frac{10\cdot9}{2}=45\). Total \(=55\) gates (+5 swaps).
Classical FFT on \(2^{10}=1024\) points: about \(N\log_2 N=1024\times10\approx10{,}240\) operations. Note: the QFT does not give you all amplitudes for free, so it must be used inside a clever algorithm.
